The word multi-brand now covers realities that have almost nothing in common. A marketplace aggregating stock from thousands of boutiques, a department store putting its floor online and a concept store building its selection season after season all share the same label, and deliver entirely different things.

That is what makes the search frustrating. The rankings available online mix the three models, and end up recommending catalogue size as though it were a measure of quality. Here is the grid we use ourselves, and what it says about each type of site.

## Three models behind one word

**The marketplace.** It does not own the stock it sells. It connects partner boutiques, often spread across several countries. The catalogue is vast, but the experience is fragmented, with separate shipments and returns terms that change from one seller to the next.

**The online department store.** It transposes the shop floor logic onto the web, with established labels and a guided journey. The retailer's reputation is reassuring, but the selection carries no identifiable stance.

**The concept store.** It buys its selection, stands behind it and builds it around a point of view. It often covers several categories, from clothing to jewellery to homeware. It is the model built for discovery, and the one willing not to sell what everyone else sells.

## The six criteria that genuinely decide

### 1. The price segment, before anything else

Established luxury, premium designer and accessible ready-to-wear rarely overlap. A premium designer piece usually sits between 100 and 600 euros depending on the category, while established luxury starts far higher. A site claiming to cover all three levels covers none of them well.

### 2. Who owns the stock

This is the most structural criterion and the least visible. A retailer who buys its pieces takes responsibility for delivery, returns and after-sales. An intermediary refers you back to the seller. The difference shows at the first problem.

### 3. Whether you can try things on

This is the structural weakness of buying fashion online, and the one decisive advantage held by houses with shops. It matters most on shoes, on structured pieces and on anything that runs small or large.

### 4. What the returns policy actually says

Three things to read before ordering, the deadline, who pays for the return, and how sale items are treated. That last point is where most bad surprises are concentrated, since many sites exclude discounted pieces from returns.

### 5. The real depth of the selection

A long designer list is not enough. What counts is the number of pieces available behind each name, and the coherence across categories. A site listing forty designers with three pieces each is not offering choice, it is offering a window display.

### 6. How often it renews

A living concept store changes names from season to season, discovers, sometimes gets it wrong, starts again. That is the sign of genuine buying work, and the difference between a selection and a frozen assortment.

## Where we stand

Our position is simple to state. We are a concept store, founded in Marseille in 2003, and we work the premium designer segment, the wardrobe that sits between mass-market ready-to-wear and established luxury.

In practice that means **238 designers** listed as of 11 September 2026, across seven categories, from clothing to jewellery, shoes to bags, through to homeware, more than 9,800 pieces in total. Established signatures such as A.P.C., Anine Bing or Isabel Marant sit alongside quieter houses, and alongside Jeanne Vouland, our own line.

We also have shops, in Marseille, Aix-en-Provence, Lyon, Saint-Tropez, Cassis and Sanary. That is what lets us answer the one real problem of online shopping, trying a piece on before keeping it. Shipping is free from 150 euros, returns are free within 14 days, and our customers rate us 4.83 out of 5 on Avis Vérifiés.

What we are not, and it is better said plainly. We are not a luxury marketplace, and for a specific piece from a major house, other sites are better placed. Our shop network is concentrated in the south-east, so the trying-on argument does not apply equally everywhere.

## What to take away

Before comparing catalogues, ask the only question that matters, are you looking for a specific piece you already know, or are you looking to discover designers you do not know yet. The first question leads to a marketplace. The second leads to a concept store.
